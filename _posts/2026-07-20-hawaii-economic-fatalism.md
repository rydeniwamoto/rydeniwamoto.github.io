> *"If a big wave comes in large fishes will come from the dark ocean which you never saw before, and when they see the small fishes they will eat them up; such also is the case with large animals, they will prey on the smaller ones. Likewise, the ships of the white man have come, and knowledgeable people have arrived from powerful countries which you have never seen before. They know our people are few in number and living in a small country; they will eat us up. Such has always been the case with large countries; the small ones have been taken over throughout the world."*
>
> — David Malo, 1837

In 1837, royal advisor David Malo wrote to Kaahumani II and Kekuanaoa on the importance and urgency of gathering knowledge and understanding of the world beyond Hawaii, a small nation of few people, to empower her ability to care for her people and defend her independence. Many Hawaiian scholars focus on the historical context of Malo's admonition. But his predictions have implications for the small fish's perception of their home near the reef and their attitude toward surviving and thriving in a sea filled with big fish.

I left Hawaii for the University of Chicago four years ago to study economics and better understand what healthy and inclusive economic development can look like back home. During my time at Chicago—well known, or rather infamous, for its historical role in developing the neoclassical academic movement known as Chicago economics (what a layperson might call conservative)—I received an incredible endowment of interdisciplinary knowledge. I understand, more than I did when I came to Chicago, how individuals respond to incentives.

With this blessing of a gift, I also received an overwhelming sense of helplessness that Hawaii would *never* be able to experience or initiate the revolutionary economic change that policymakers, local business owners, and locals are hoping for. It is not that Hawaii will never experience economic development of *any kind*. I do not believe our economic conditions have become that dire. It is instead that Hawaii would *never* have the economic development needed to reverse the current economic trajectory choking and pricing out locals.

## Economic fatalism and how it comes to be

After some prescriptive discussions with my peers, I've come to call this feeling economic fatalism. Much like the philosophy of fatalism, which refers to the belief that all things have been predetermined, leaving humans with no genuine control or agency over the course of their own fate, economic fatalism is the belief that all locally originating economic growth possibilities are predetermined and constrained by a place's natural endowment. I believe the more one learns about orthodox economics—the economics taught and researched in most of the world's universities—the more susceptible one is to the allure of economic fatalism.

The simplest explanation of this jargon is this: we cannot have our cake and eat it. Consider the question troubling innumerable families across Hawaii: "Why is housing so expensive?" In learning how economies develop and cities grow, you come to learn of the urban amenity premium—the additional "price" residents of a city are willing to pay (via a higher real cost of living) to live in cities with better amenities like parks, views, weather, infrastructure, and so on.[^1] One [2013 paper](https://rossihansberg.economics.uchicago.edu/UAWApp.pdf) attempting to quantify the premiums (via population change) in cities around the United States estimated that Honolulu, if it had average amenities, would lose approximately 40% of its population and have 21% lower rents.[^2] But we cannot pick up our islands and move them to the golden, yet seasonally frigid fields of the Midwest, nor can we extend our shores and block public access to beaches. This rent difference is simply a consequence of the blessing of our natural endowment, and we will always have to pay this premium so long as our endowment remains unchanged. To a rational economic agent, the actionable solution to these high costs of living is binary: accept them and relish in the warm sun and salty trade winds or reject them and move away.

## The trajectory of development in Hawaii

Hawaii's economic history is one aligned with economic fatalism.[^3] Following the unification of the islands under Kamehameha, most of the kingdom's economic prosperity in the early 19th century came from the export of its endowed resources: first sandalwood, then whaling. American businessmen in Hawaii, closely following the agricultural model in the Philippines and Cuba, then Spanish colonies, established the sugarcane and pineapple plantations which would later lead to the overthrow of the monarchy and the annexation of Hawaii. At the height of American military power, defense and military investments in the newly acquired territory served as a supplementary form of economic activity. Decades later, public and private diversification attempts shifted Hawaii's economy away from agriculture and toward tourism: exporting our culture and natural beauty rather than resources grown from the land itself.

This is a truncated economic narrative of Hawaii, but the aim of this essay is not to discuss the economic history of Hawaii but instead demonstrate how readily orthodox economics can explain Hawaii's economic trajectory. The fact of the matter is that whether by fate or a lack of creativity, all of these historical landmarks resulted from the natural endowment of our islands.

In recent years, we have tried and continue to try to diversify our economy away from defense and tourism to knowledge-centric sectors. The number and scope of these expansion efforts are admirable, from local manufacturing and retail (e.g., ALOHA collection, Mana Up, etc.) to technology (e.g., OceanIt, Hawaii Technology Development Corporation) to finance (e.g., Blue Startups). But admiration is more an assessment of sentiment than of outcomes. Orthodox economics tells us we do not have the population, business density, nor natural endowment to achieve the development necessary to initiate an economic revolution to draw down housing prices or rents nor raise the pay for the people who need it most.

## So we gon' make die dead, or what?

I haven't fully reconciled my own attitude toward economic fatalism, but I think there are three ways to address it—only two of which are likely to yield productive paths forward. The first is that economic fatalism only maintains its appeal so long as the models it follows from are valid; the second is the compound effect of small, community-centric efforts on significant advances in development; the third is the possibility of going against orthodox economics to produce the change locals want and need.

The first solution, dismissing economic fatalism on the grounds of a false premise, is not unfounded but it is unproductive. One might critique "Western" economic models and their validity, arguing they are reductions or abstractions of reality that inappropriately model humans—who are individually unique and unpredictable—as predictable mathematical and statistical functions. But humans, in aggregate, are as predictable as they are unpredictable. The adage goes, "All models are wrong, but some are useful," and we take this to mean some are informative. Dismissing informative economic models and their consequences on the basis they are wrong is one valid perspective, but it certainly does not lead to the action we need. It is therefore not productive.

A promising pathway comes from the compounding effect of small, community-centric efforts toward a shared vision for the economic future. Efforts in this area are often what locals call "bringing back the spirit of old Hawaii," a time when neighbors cared, shared fruit, cooked, and told stories with one another. It is possible, albeit difficult. Community-centric efforts are characterized by their detachment from a striving for power or profit—easy to discern from the outside but difficult to identify from the inside. One exciting example of these efforts is taking place in Waianae, where several non-profit organizations, small businesses, and farms are working to collectively create jobs and increase the productive capacity of local agriculture to reduce Hawaii's dependence on imported produce.

Finally, and most difficult, are efforts that advance the economic well-being of locals in ways that go against orthodox economics (e.g., if Elon Musk chose to [end world hunger](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/11/elon-musk-un-world-hunger-famine/) for \$6 billion). These unorthodox solutions are what we might call "Schrödinger's development" in that we cannot know whether it exists or not without observing it first. Whereas community-centric development efforts are detached from a striving for power or profit, unorthodox efforts may require sacrifices of power and profit, possibly in the short- and long-term, to accomplish some collective goal. It may feel silly to describe efforts that may or may not exist (hence why my description is wishy-washy), but the possibility of it existing is enough to warrant a mention.

What does this mean for economic development in Hawaii? Are all of our efforts today futile? I do not think economic development is a hopeless cause in Hawaii. The fact that we are able to adapt and adjust to changes we face—Pearl Harbor, Japan's Lost Decade, 9/11, the Great Financial Crisis, COVID-19, and so on—serves as a testament to our resilience and strength as a people and as an economy. But what we, locals, want is something beyond resilience or robustness. We want antifragility, and accomplishing that will require revolutionary economic change that we can achieve only by working around economic fatalism.
