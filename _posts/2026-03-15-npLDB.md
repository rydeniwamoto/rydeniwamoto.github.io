There is an emerging literature in behavioral economics on left-digit bias (LDB),
which is the tendency for individuals to place undue emphasis on the left-most digit(s)
of a number in decisionmaking. This behavior has been observed and demonstrated in consumers
(e.g., [Lyft riders](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/6/3186/7045819),
[retailers](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/5/2612/6931812), etc.),
firms, medical practitioners (e.g., [kidney donation screening](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/ctr.14284?casa_token=trFIO130GzgAAAAA%3AuHanSy2MuKI_xVpSTYJ4mwONwBosbKxweC4zrc-DNZ4oR_S_Glbs7kQa1Uk7L6N-MMsHjeBJox_Q)),
and financial professionals (e.g., [traders](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268123003645)).

For practitioners (perhaps at large firms like Amazon, Uber, Walmart, etc.) looking
to analyze this behavior in markets (e.g., in the supply or demand of certain products or services),
several methodological challenges arise:
1. **Causal identification of the slope parameter**: As tends to be the case in the econometric
study of supply or demand, causal identification of the slope parameter (that is,
"true" slope of the supply or demand curve) is difficult in observational settings
due to the simultaneity problem. Outside of a randomized or experimental setting,
credible identification requires an instrumental variables-flavored argument, whether
through a typical instrument or a demand or supply shifter (depending on what slope parameter is
being estimated).
2. **Causal identification of the discontinuity parameter**: In settings where products
are a collection of characteristics, causal identification of the discontinuity requires
justification via selection on observable confounders. For instance, in "Left Digit Bias
at Lyft," List and coauthors verify trips above and below dollar cutoffs do not differ
significantly in their characteristics (e.g., distance, time, etc.). This task can be difficult
when the researcher is uncertain of the data-generating process or if there are many
covariates which significantly affect prices and/or behavior above and below the
integer cutoff.
3. **Computational burden**: Access to large swaths of data is both a blessing and a curse.
On one hand, it permits the researcher to answer an incredibly diverse array of questions
most academics could only dream about. On the other hand, it is a slippery slope to
non-rigorous or non-robust analyses. This trap is no less applicable to LDB research than it is
any other field of behavioral science. Consider, for instance, List and coauthors' justification
for their two-stage least squares methodology: "We adopt the IV approach described above in [Section
5.3] because 2SLS can be easily implemented in SQL code, which allows us to run our regressions
on datasets with hundreds of millions of observations." Granted, they verify their results
against an experimental benchmark. Perhaps it is just unpleasant for people to show you their
underwear. Alternatively, in the realm of non-robustness, we may also be concerned in the difference in
how global methods like splines estimate parameters relative to local ones like
local polynomial regression.

My primary interest here is precisely this point: to explore the extent to which we observe
threats to robustness under different methodologies in the big data regime. Motivated by
[Gelman's (2018)](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/08/02/38160/#:~:text=The%20paper's%20key%20graph%20shows%20that%20the,recommended%20in%20some%20of%20the%20econometrics%20literature)
critique of [Chen et al. (2017)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1616784114), I simulate a case of
supply-side left-digit bias with a fixed discontinuity size but varying levels of curvature (i.e.,
I use a parameter \\(\gamma\\) to alter the functional form of the latent supply curve, where \\(\gamma = 0\\)
corresponds to linearity, \\(\gamma < 0\\) convexity, and \\(\gamma > 0\\) concavity) to test
how different non-parametric methods fare at estimating the discontinuity at the integer
cutoff.

While this exploration is relevant both in the "normal" (i.e., \\(n \gg p\\)) and big data regime,
it is particularly relevant in the latter. Because popular software packages like [`rdrobust`](https://rdpackages.github.io/rdrobust/) in R
cannot parse large datasets efficiently, global methods like splines or kink-discontinuity linear
regression are tempting. For an idea of *how* tempting such methods can be, a greedy economist with
biblical levels of compute utilization processing data with some 50-80K rows with `rdrobust`
would have to wait around 30-50 minutes for one RD estimate, supposing a bandwidth has already been
selected. In the LDB setting, where this process must be repeated at every integer (e.g., from all
dollar values from $8 to $43) with hundreds of thousands, and sometimes millions, of observations at
each cutoff, this wait time is extended substantially. This does not even consider confidence interval
estimation, which is often done via subsampling or bootstrapping.

## Setup

We consider the following setting: at an integer cutoff \\(c\\), fix a discontinuity
size and latent supply curve slope. The researcher attempts to estimate the inattention parameter
\\(\theta\\) using two different methods for the **discontinuity**:
1. **`rdrobust` (local polynomial regression)**: Using the `rdrobust` package from [Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik](https://rdpackages.github.io/rdrobust/),
estimate the discontinuity at the integer using local polynomial regression
on both sides of the cutoff. The specific bandwidth selection method is not particularly important
(e.g., MSE-optimal vs. coverage error minimizing bandwidth) in the context of interest, so we
opt for MSE-optimal bandwidth selection for simplicity. We test both local linear (\\(p = 1\\))
and local quadratic (\\(p = 2\\)) fits.
2. **Kink-discontinuity regression (discontinuous linear splines)**: As in the big data economist's
siren setting from above, instead use kink-discontinuity (linear) regression to estimate the
discontinuity at the integer cutoff:
$$
y_i \sim \left(1, \; P_i, \; \boldsymbol{1}\{P_i \geq c\}, \; \boldsymbol{1}\{P_i \geq c\} \cdot (P_i - c)\right).
$$
We consider two variants of this method: a global version which runs the regression on all data 
before and after the integer cutoff, and a windowed version which restricts to observations within 
\\(\pm 0.50\\) of the cutoff.

I assume the researcher has access data generated experimentally analogous to [List et al. (2023)](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/6/3186/7045819), 
which leverages a random multiplier instrument \\(M\\) to exogenously vary the price of rides
offered to riders (i.e., \\(p_{\mathrm{final}} = Mp_{\mathrm{original}}\\)), so we
use 2SLS to estimate a common estimate of the latent curve of interest. 
[Proposition 2](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/6/3186/7045819) of List et al.,
Because the multiplier variation spans cent values uniformly, the 2SLS slope estimates the
average slope over a dollar-wide range, which recovers the latent slope \\(\beta\\). The
inattention parameter is then:

$$
\hat\theta = \frac{\hat\Delta}{\hat\beta^{2SLS}},
$$

where \\(\hat\Delta\\) is the discontinuity estimate (from either method above) and
\\(\hat\beta_{2SLS}\\) is the IV slope. Since both methods share the same denominator,
any difference in the estimate of \\(\theta\\) is driven entirely by the choice of
discontinuity estimator.

## Theoretical framework
Recall the perceived price under LDB follows the inattention model posed by
[Lacetera, Pope, and Sydnor (2012)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.102.5.2206):

$$
\hat{p}(p; \theta) = \lfloor p \rfloor + (1 - \theta) \cdot (p - \lfloor p \rfloor),
$$

where \\(\theta \in [0, 1]\\) dictates the degree of inattention. \\(\theta = 0\\)
corresponds to true price perception (no exhibition of LDB) while \\(\theta = 1\\)
corresponds to completely imperfect price imperception (complete exhibition of LDB; 
agents round down entirely to the dollar). Outcomes (e.g., demand or supply) depend on 
perceived price through a latent curve which we allow to be nonlinear through the 
curvature parameter \\(\gamma\\):

$$
Y_i = \alpha + \beta \cdot \hat{p}(P_i; \theta) + \gamma \cdot \hat{p}(P_i; \theta)^2 + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2).
$$

Note \\(\gamma = 0\\) produces a linear latent curve and the kink-discontinuity regression is correctly specified.
However, as \\(|\gamma|\\) grows, the piecewise-linear assumption becomes increasingly strict and incorrect, 
and the curvature may bleed into, and thus bias, the estimated discontinuity.

Prices with exogenous variation are generated in a similar manner to the Lyft design. Baseline prices
\\(P^{cf} \sim \text{Uniform}(c - h, c + h)\\) are multiplied by a random factor
\\(M \sim \text{Uniform}(0.9, 1.1)\\), giving observed prices \\(P = P^{cf} \cdot M\\).
The half-width \\(h\\) controls how spread out the data is around the cutoff. In our
baseline specification, \\(h = 1\\) with cutoff \\(c = 1\\), so counterfactual prices
are drawn from \\(U(0, 2)\\) — spanning the single integer boundary at \\$1.00.

For the simulations below, I fix \\(\alpha = 10\\), \\(\beta = -5\\), \\(\sigma = 1\\),
and \\(N = 5{,}000\\). I vary the true
\\(\theta \in \{0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75\}\\), the curvature
\\(\gamma \in \{0, 2, 5\}\\), and the price range. To keep prices non-negative,
the half-width \\(h\\) is paired with the cutoff \\(c\\):
the baseline specification uses \\(h = 1, c = 1\\) (prices on \\((0, 2)\\), single
cutoff at \\$1), and the wide specification uses \\(h = 3, c = 5\\) (prices on
\\((2, 8)\\), multiple cutoffs). Each cell is simulated 200 times.

## Results

### When data spans a single cutoff, the shortcut is free

Under the baseline price range (\\(h = 1\\)), counterfactual prices are drawn from
\\(U(0, 2)\\) and observed prices (after the multiplier) span roughly \\([0, 2.2]\\)
— covering the single integer cutoff at \\(P = 1\\). In this regime,
the global kink-discontinuity regression and `rdrobust` produce nearly identical
estimates of \\(\theta\\). Under the linear DGP (\\(\gamma = 0\\)):

| Method | \\(\theta = 0.10\\) | \\(\theta = 0.25\\) | \\(\theta = 0.50\\) | \\(\theta = 0.75\\) |
|--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Kink-disc (global) | 0.002 | 0.001 | 0.004 | 0.002 |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 1\\)) | 0.002 | 0.004 | -0.004 | 0.002 |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 2\\)) | -0.004 | 0.004 | -0.003 | 0.006 |

*Table: Bias in \\(\hat\theta\\) under linear DGP (\\(\gamma = 0\\), \\(h = 1\\), \\(c = 1\\), \\(N = 5{,}000\\)).*

All three are essentially unbiased. If anything, the kink-discontinuity regression
has slightly *lower* RMSE (0.014 at \\(\theta = 0.10\\) versus 0.024 for `rdrobust`
\\(p=1\\)) because it uses all the data in a correctly specified parametric model. In this
setting, the big data economist's shortcut is genuinely free: you get lower variance,
faster computation, and no bias penalty.

### Curvature hurts everyone equally when data spans a single cutoff

Introduce moderate curvature (\\(\gamma = 2\\)). The latent supply curve is now
\\(Y = 10 - 5\hat{p} + 2\hat{p}^2 + \varepsilon\\), so the local slope at the cutoff
(\\(\hat{p} \approx 1\\)) is \\(-5 + 4 = -1\\), substantially flatter than the
average slope \\(\beta = -5\\) which the 2SLS recovers. Every method develops
positive bias in the range of 0.05 to 0.08:

| Method | \\(\theta = 0.10\\) | \\(\theta = 0.25\\) | \\(\theta = 0.50\\) | \\(\theta = 0.75\\) |
|--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Kink-disc (global) | 0.071 | 0.079 | 0.072 | 0.046 |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 1\\)) | 0.068 | 0.074 | 0.053 | 0.058 |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 2\\)) | 0.022 | 0.076 | 0.060 | 0.041 |

*Table: Bias under moderate curvature (\\(\gamma = 2\\), \\(h = 1\\), \\(c = 1\\)).*

No method clearly dominates. The explanation is simple: when data spans a single
cutoff, even the "local" estimator's bandwidth encompasses essentially the
same observations as the global regression. And the bias is not primarily
from the discontinuity estimate — it comes from the shared IV slope, which estimates
a linear average (\\(\hat\beta_{2SLS} \approx -5\\)) over a region where the curve is
bending. The denominator is the bottleneck, not the numerator.

Under strong curvature (\\(\gamma = 5\\)), the pattern persists but with negative
bias on the order of 0.02–0.12, again spread equally across methods. The
curvature is so extreme that the quadratic term dominates the linear term near the
cutoff, and the IV slope's linear approximation overshoots the true local effect.
`rdrobust` with \\(p = 2\\) buys slightly better coverage (70% versus 47% for the
kink-disc) because its confidence intervals are wider, but the point estimates are
no better.

### Wide price ranges expose the global method

Now widen the price range to \\(h = 3\\) with cutoff \\(c = 5\\), so counterfactual
prices are drawn from \\(U(2, 8)\\) and observed prices cover integer boundaries at
\\(P = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7\\). This is the setting closest to real-world LDB analyses, where
a researcher pools data across a range of dollar values and estimates a single
kink-discontinuity regression.

Even under perfect linearity (\\(\gamma = 0\\)), the global kink-discontinuity regression
collapses. With \\(\theta = 0.50\\):

| Method | Bias | RMSE | Coverage |
|--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Kink-disc (global) | **-0.374** | **0.379** | **1.5%** |
| Kink-disc (windowed) | -0.073 | 1.757 | 99.5% |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 1\\)) | -0.165 | 2.394 | 97.5% |
| `rdrobust` (\\(p = 2\\)) | **0.020** | **0.251** | **98.5%** |

*Table: Performance under wide price range (\\(h = 3\\), \\(c = 5\\), \\(\gamma = 0\\)).*

The global kink-discontinuity estimator absorbs a bias of nearly \\(-0.37\\) — it
estimates \\(\hat\theta \approx 0.13\\) when the truth is 0.50 — with coverage
collapsing to 1.5%. The cause is mechanical: the piecewise-linear regression only
models a discontinuity at \\(P = 5\\), but the data contains discontinuities at every
integer. The unmodeled jumps at \\(P = 3, 4, 6, 7\\) contaminate the slope and
intercept coefficients, dragging the estimated discontinuity at \\(P = 5\\) away from
its true value.

The windowed kink-disc (restricting to \\(\pm 0.50\\) around the cutoff)
avoids this contamination but pays a steep variance penalty: most of the data is
discarded, and the remaining observations yield noisy estimates (RMSE \\(\approx 1.76\\)).
`rdrobust` with \\(p = 1\\) shows a similar variance blowup. The clear winner is
`rdrobust` with \\(p = 2\\) — the local quadratic fit handles both the
discontinuity and any local curvature, with bias of just 0.02 and RMSE of 0.25.

When curvature is added on top of the wide price range (\\(\gamma \geq 2\\),
\\(h = 3\\), \\(c = 5\\)), all methods fail catastrophically: bias exceeds \\(-0.50\\) across the
board with zero coverage. This is not a failure of any particular discontinuity
estimator — the shared IV slope, estimated via 2SLS over a wide quadratic range,
is so far from the true local slope at the cutoff that no discontinuity estimate
can save you. The denominator of \\(\hat\theta\\) is the binding constraint.

## Discussion

The results suggest a simple decision rule for practitioners:

1. **If your data is already concentrated near a single integer cutoff** (e.g., you
have pre-filtered to a narrow price band, or you are analyzing a single dollar
threshold), the kink-discontinuity regression is a perfectly adequate substitute
for `rdrobust`. It is faster, simpler, and — when correctly specified — slightly
more precise. The computational convenience is genuinely free.

2. **If your data spans a wide price range** encompassing multiple integer boundaries,
the global kink-discontinuity regression is badly misspecified even under linearity.
You either need to window your data carefully (accepting a variance cost) or use a
local estimator like `rdrobust` with \\(p \geq 2\\). This is the setting most
relevant to practitioners at firms like Lyft or Amazon, who are pooling ride or
transaction data across a range of dollar values.

3. **Curvature in the latent curve is an equal-opportunity problem.** When the true
supply or demand relationship is meaningfully nonlinear, neither local nor global
methods for the discontinuity can save you if the slope estimate (the denominator
of \\(\hat\theta\\)) is estimated linearly over a region where the curve bends. This
is a limitation of the identification strategy itself, not of any particular software
package. The solution is to ensure the slope is estimated locally as well — perhaps
via a local IV or by restricting the 2SLS to a narrow price band — but that is a
separate (and harder) problem.

The broader lesson, consistent with [Gelman's (2018)](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2018/08/02/38160/#:~:text=The%20paper's%20key%20graph%20shows%20that%20the,recommended%20in%20some%20of%20the%20econometrics%20literature)
critique, is that global parametric methods can be seductive in large-data settings
precisely because they appear to work: they produce tight standard errors, the
coefficients look reasonable, and the code runs in seconds. But the tightness of
those standard errors can mask substantial bias when the parametric assumptions
are wrong. In the LDB context, the most dangerous form of this is not curvature in
the latent demand curve — which at least affects all methods symmetrically — but
the presence of multiple unmodeled discontinuities in a pooled regression. That
failure mode is silent (the regression runs without complaint), large (bias of
\\(0.37\\) in \\(\hat\theta\\) under our baseline calibration), and entirely avoidable
with a local estimator.
